## Q1. Price to beat (Amazon AU and other AU retail anchors, two tiers, replacement filters)

### Takeaway
There is no gap at the bottom of the market. The cheapest comparable new HEPA purifiers on Amazon AU cost **A$63–99 for small rooms** and **A$89.99–149.90 for medium rooms (CADR 170–370 m³/h)**, all with free 1–3-day delivery to Sydney (checked 2026-10-10). Branded anchors sit at the same prices: **Philips 600i (CADR 170 m³/h, 44 m², app) A$129** sold by Amazon AU with "2K+ bought in past month", Levoit Core Mini A$99 and Levoit Core 300 A$169. Branded Wi-Fi units drop further on sale; the Philips 1000i (CADR 300) went to A$141.50 on 29 Sep 2026. Applying the 10–20% rule gives about **A$50–80 for small rooms**, which is below the A$100–400 band, and **A$112–126 for a CADR ≈300–370 m³/h Wi-Fi unit**, against Kmart Anko Large A$149 and Aldi's past A$99.99–149 Special Buys. Replacement filters are already sold as cheap compatibles for every popular model.

### Inferences
- **Small tier:** the comparable floor is HIFRESH A$63.17 (CADR 80) to Honeyuan/LOREYISOL A$88.99–89.99. A strict 10–20% discount gives **A$50–80**, below the A$100–400 brief. Levoit Core Mini (A$99, Vesync AU warranty) and Philips 600i (A$129, app) cap any no-name small unit near A$70–90 even before the rule. The small tier fails the price band.
- **Medium tier without app (CADR ≈200–300):** VEWIOR A$89.99 (CADR 170), Lenoxx A$109.95 (CADR 205) and Lefame A$139.99 (CADR 300) give a target of **A$89–119**.
- **Medium tier with Wi-Fi/app (CADR ≈300–370):** MORENTO A$139.99 (CADR 340, sold by a Foshan company direct) and Advwin A$149.90 (CADR 370) give a target of **A$112–126**. Philips 600i at A$129 and the 1000i on sale at A$141.50–149 sit right on top of that. Kmart Anko Large is A$149, with a claimed CADR of 323 m³/h (search summary). The only tier inside the A$100–400 band is therefore a CADR ≈300+ m³/h unit priced at about **A$119**.
- **Filters:** compatible filters at A$17–35 exist for every popular model, so recurring filter revenue would come only from a proprietary filter shape. That leaves the importer with filter-stock risk, and a Marketplace one-off buyer is unlikely to come back. Treat filter sales as upside (perhaps A$10–15 GP per filter, an estimate), not as the business case.

## Q2. Demand in Australia, buyer trust, and bulky-goods/pickup advantage

### Takeaway
Demand is real and repeats each year, with spikes: steady in spring (hay fever and thunderstorm asthma, late September–December), and sharp in bushfire-smoke summers, when stores ran out in 2019–20. In October 2026 Amazon AU is selling hundreds to thousands of branded units a month. But there is no pickup advantage. Every medium-room carton found weighs 2.5–7.8 kg and measures 0.02–0.08 m³, far inside Australia Post's 22 kg / 105 cm / 0.25 m³ limits, so Amazon, Kmart and others deliver free in 1–3 days. A no-name unit sold for a health purpose on Marketplace competes with branded units that have thousands of reviews and Vesync/Philips warranties at the same price. I found no direct data on whether Marketplace buyers trust such units.

### Inferences
- **There is no bulky-goods edge.** The largest tower carton (≈0.077 m³, 7.8 kg) is about a third of Australia Post's volume limit and a third of its weight limit. Every competitor ships free and fast. A Marketplace seller's only levers are price, and same-day pickup on smoke days when online stock sells out (seen in 2019–20). That second lever is episodic and cannot be planned for.
- **Seasonality cuts both ways.** Spring and summer are good selling windows, and a bushfire-smoke season can clear stock quickly. A mild, wet summer leaves a small importer holding stock while branded units are discounted (Philips 1000i A$141.50, Levoit A$169 in July and September 2026, per Q1). Landing stock by mid-September for spring/summer sales means placing the order by late July (assumption: about 35 days production plus about 3–4 weeks sea and clearance).
- **Health-claim trust is the main demand risk (inference).** A buyer can get a Philips (Air+ app, 2-year brand support) for A$129 or a Levoit for A$99–169 with free next-day delivery and Amazon returns. That same buyer is unlikely to pay A$99–119 cash to a Marketplace stranger for an unknown brand making H13/CADR health claims, unless the price gap is wide or a smoke event has emptied the shelves. Ozone/ioniser models should be avoided entirely.

## Q3. Compliance: EESS level, RCM/ACMA, Wi-Fi radio + cyber, ACL claims (HEPA/CADR/ozone), certificate re-use and costs

### Takeaway
A plain mains-powered HEPA room purifier is **EESS Level 1**, not Level 3. The current EESS list (v4.3, July 2024, aligned to AS/NZS 4417.2:2020) does not mention air purifiers, air cleaners or AS/NZS 60335.2.65. The "Fan" definition covers only appliances whose primary function is "moving air … for comfort or extraction". Level 1 needs no certificate and no equipment registration. It does need a registered Responsible Supplier (A$239.74/yr), RCM marking and English test evidence held for five years.
- **Caveat 1:** many cheap and smart purifiers run on DC 24 V from an external plug-pack, and that adapter is a Level 3 "power supply". It needs a certificate holder's authorisation and an A$89.85/yr registration. The integral plug/cord also needs a valid Certificate of Conformity on file.
- **Caveat 2:** "purifying fans", heater-purifiers and warm-mist or refrigerant combos fall into Level 3 categories (inference).
- **Wi-Fi/app models** add ACMA radio and EMC records, and, if manufactured on or after 4 Mar 2026, a manufacturer-prepared cyber-security statement of compliance that the supplier must pass on.
- **Claims:** "HEPA H13" and "CADR" are ACL s.29(1)(a)/(g) representations, so the seller needs EN 1822/ISO 29463 filter-class and CADR test reports. No ACCC action specific to purifiers was found. The TGA has fined COVID-era "virus-killing" device advertising.

### Cited Findings
**EESS risk level (primary documents)**
- The EESS "Relevant Documents" page links the current Level 2/3 list as "EESS-Inscope-Equipment-Definitions-and-Risk-Levels-v4.3-Approved.pdf" ("Current: July 2024, version 4.3 – aligns to AS/NZS 4417.2:2020 edition"). — [EESS Relevant Documents](https://www.eess.gov.au/about/relevant-documents/); [EESS v4.3 PDF](https://www.eess.gov.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/EESS-Inscope-Equipment-Definitions-and-Risk-Levels-v4.3-Approved.pdf)
- A text search of v4.3 (and v4.2) finds **no** entry for "air purifier", "air cleaner", "ioniser", "ozone" or AS/NZS 60335.2.65. "Humidif…" appears only within "Liquid heating appliance" (heats liquid for humidifying, ≤10 L, Level 3) and the air-conditioner entries (dehumidifying). — [EESS v4.3](https://www.eess.gov.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/EESS-Inscope-Equipment-Definitions-and-Risk-Levels-v4.3-Approved.pdf) (my search of the PDF text)
- **Fan (Level 3):** "An electrical appliance that— (a) is a household type; (b) has a primary function of moving air in its vicinity for comfort or extraction purposes; and (c) is self-contained and includes— (d) duct fans; (e) any associated ancillary equipment", class specification AS/NZS 60335.2.80; "Fans that blow a mist of water … are included". — [EESS v4.3](https://www.eess.gov.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/EESS-Inscope-Equipment-Definitions-and-Risk-Levels-v4.3-Approved.pdf)
- Precedent for "contains a fan ≠ Fan": the evaporative cooler definition says it "incorporates a fan for moving air", yet "Portable evaporative coolers are level 1 as they are not fixed devices". — same document
- **Default rule:** "Risk Level 1 – electrical equipment that is classified low risk or unknown risk and is any in-scope electrical equipment not classified as Risk Level 3 or Risk Level 2." — [EESS In-Scope Electrical Equipment](https://www.eess.gov.au/equipment/risk-level-definition/). In scope means rated >50 V AC or >120 V ripple-free DC and <1000 V AC/1500 V DC, household use. — same page
- **Level 1 obligations:** the Responsible Supplier must be registered and declare compliance. It "must hold and have ready access to evidence the equipment meets relevant standards … made available to regulators upon request within 10 business days". "Examples of documentary evidence include manufacturer's test reports, test reports conducted by an independent testing laboratory or a valid Australian Certificate of Suitability". Evidence is retained five years. "Level 1 can be listed in the EESS Registration Database, however registration is voluntary". A Certificate of Suitability is voluntary. RCM marking is required per AS/NZS 4417.1/.2. For "plugs, cords and plug/cord/connectors (cord sets) sold as an integral part of other equipment … current and valid Certificates of Conformity must be held by the Responsible Supplier". — [EESS Level 1](https://www.eess.gov.au/registration/registration-in-scope-electrical-equipment/level-1/)
- "For In-scope electrical equipment Level 1 and 2, testing can be conducted by any testing body or in-house test facility, but it is recommended to use an accredited testing body". Level 3 needs accredited labs, and CB reports must come with CB certificates. — [EESS Testing and Testing Laboratories](https://www.eess.gov.au/safety-requirements/testing-and-testing-laboratories/)
- Relevant standard for Level 1: the AS/AS-NZS standard specific to the equipment together with AS/NZS 3820; failing that, the IEC standard with AS/NZS 3820. — [EESS Relevant Standard](https://www.eess.gov.au/safety-requirements/relevant-standard/). AS/NZS 60335.2.65:2024 (air-cleaning appliances) supersedes the 2015 edition three years after publication; both are current meanwhile. — [ANSI webstore listing](https://webstore.ansi.org/standards/sai/nzs60335652024) (search summary). IEC 60335-2-65 does not apply to appliances that intentionally emit harmful radiation, and a Canadian adoption excludes appliances designed to generate ozone intentionally. — [IECEE](https://www.iecee.org/certification/iec-standards/iec-60335-2-652023); [SCC](https://scc-ccn.ca/standardsdb/standards/4026203) (search summaries)
- **Power supply or charger (Level 3):** "(a) provides an output not exceeding 50V AC. or 120V ripple free DC; and … (c) is a household type for either charging batteries or to provide supply to separate equipment"; class spec AS/NZS 61558.2.16 (switch mode). **Plug** and **Supply flexible cord** are also Level 3 entries. — [EESS v4.3](https://www.eess.gov.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/EESS-Inscope-Equipment-Definitions-and-Risk-Levels-v4.3-Approved.pdf)
- **Adapter-powered purifiers are common.** Advwin's Wi-Fi unit lists "Adapter Output: 24V 2A … Package Content: … 1 x Adapter" and uses the Tuya/Smart Life app. — [Amazon AU B0FPC1G3PY](https://www.amazon.com.au/dp/B0FPC1G3PY). VEWIOR includes "1x Adapter". — [Amazon AU B0F4RBRJTP](https://www.amazon.com.au/dp/B0F4RBRJTP). - Level 3 duties (if an adapter is supplied): Australian/NZ Certificate of Conformity plus registration linked to the Responsible Supplier. A Responsible Supplier may use another holder's certificate only with "permission to use this certificate … from the certificate holder" (declaration wording). Real example: the PSU maker Phihong issues authorisation letters to Australian importers. — [EESS v4.3 preface](https://www.eess.gov.au/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/EESS-Inscope-Equipment-Definitions-and-Risk-Levels-v4.3-Approved.pdf); [EESS Responsible Supplier Declarations](https://www.eess.gov.au/responsible-supplier/responsible-supplier-declarations/); [Phihong letter](https://www.phihong.com/wp-content/uploads/RCMAA10S-050A-EESS-A1.pdf) (via vertical_appliances_emobility.md)

**ACMA (EMC and radio)**
- ACMA compliance levels: level 1 "testing is not required but you may choose to obtain a test report"; level 2 "testing is required and you can use a testing body"; level 3 "testing is required by an accredited testing body". A declaration of conformity (Form C02 content) must be completed before applying the RCM. Records required: declaration, test reports, product description. — [ACMA Step 2: show your product complies](https://www.acma.gov.au/step-2-show-your-product-complies); [ACMA Form C02](https://www.acma.gov.au/publications/2019-11/form/form-c02-suppliers-declaration-conformity) (search summaries; acma.gov.au robots.txt was unreachable, so not fetched). **Cyber security (Wi-Fi/app models)**
- Cyber Security (Security Standards for Smart Devices) Rules 2025: Part 2 and Schedule 1 commence **4 March 2026**. A statement of compliance "must be prepared by, or on behalf of, the manufacturer". It must include product type and batch identifier; names and addresses of the manufacturer, its authorised representative and any Australian authorised representatives; declarations of compliance; "the defined support period"; and signature, place and date. Retention is 5 years. Schedule 1 requires passwords to be unique per product or user-defined (not incremental, serial-derived or guessable), published security-issue reporting information, and defined support periods. — [F2025L00276](https://legislation.gov.au/F2025L00276/asmade/2025-03-04/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_1/document_1.html)
- Home Affairs factsheet: the standards apply to products "manufactured on and from 4 March 2026"; products made earlier are not required to comply. "Suppliers must supply in-scope products with a statement of compliance, where the supplier is aware, or could reasonably be expected to be aware, that the product will be acquired by a consumer in Australia". Manufacturers and suppliers keep it five years. Enforcement runs from Technology Assessment and Regulation Office engagement to compliance notice to stop notice. Therapeutic goods are excluded. — [Home Affairs factsheet (PDF)](https://www.homeaffairs.gov.au/cyber-security-subsite/files/factsheet-security-standards-for-smart-devices-and-consumer-grade-rules.pdf)

**Claims law (ACL) and enforcement history**

Ch 5 Div 2 s.219: "Regulator may require claims to be substantiated". s.7(1)(e): an importer is a "manufacturer" if the actual manufacturer "does not have a place of business in Australia". — [Competition and Consumer Act 2010, Sch 2 (legislation.gov.au, compilation 27 May 2026)](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00109/2026-05-27/2026-05-27/text/original/epub/OEBPS/document_4/document_4.html)

- **HEPA class evidence:** under EN 1822, H13 (= ISO 29463 ISO 35) requires ≥99.95% efficiency at the most penetrating particle size (MPPS). H14 requires ≥99.995%. ### Inferences
**EESS level by product configuration** (my reading of the definitions above; confirm with a certifier):

| Configuration | EESS treatment | What the importer needs |
|---|---|---|
| Mains-cord HEPA purifier (internal PSU), no heater/humidifier | **Level 1** (not listed; not a "Fan" because its primary function is air cleaning) | RS registration A$239.74/yr; RCM label; test evidence to AS/NZS 60335.1 + 60335.2.65 (+ AS/NZS 3820); copy of the cord-set/plug CoC; no equipment registration, no certificate |
| Same, powered by an external 12/24 V adapter | Purifier body = ELV (out of EESS scope); **adapter = Level 3 power supply** | As above, plus a CoC for the adapter used with the holder's permission, and Level 3 registration A$89.85/yr in the importer's name |
| "Purifying fan" / bladeless purifier-fan sold for cooling | Likely **Level 3 Fan** (primary function includes comfort airflow) | AU CoC + registration |
| Heater-purifier (hot + cool) | **Level 3 Room heater** | AU CoC + registration |
| Warm-mist humidifier-purifier | **Level 3 Liquid heating appliance** | AU CoC + registration |
| R290 dehumidifier-purifier | **Level 3 AC with flammable refrigerant** (dehumidifying) | AU CoC + registration |

- **Certificate re-use:** Level 1 has no certificate to borrow. The importer needs the factory to hand over its full test reports (IEC/AS-NZS 60335-2-65 safety report with AU national differences, EMC CISPR 14-1, RF report for Wi-Fi) in English, tied to the exact model. A factory's "SAA" claim on a Level 1 purifier is at most a voluntary Certificate of Suitability or a certificate for its cord, plug or adapter. Any Level 3 certificate (adapter, cord set) can be used by a different importer only with the holder's written permission (confirmed by the declaration wording). Otherwise the certificate can be transferred (SAA Approvals charges A$350 + GST) or a new one obtained (A$800 + GST plus testing).
- **Scenario costs used in Q5 (estimates):**
  - **Scenario A** (factory/adapter-maker documents usable): A$239.74 RS + A$500 document review (assumption) + A$89.85 adapter registration if applicable = **A$740–830**.
  - **Scenario B** (new testing at an accredited lab): add **A$6,000** safety + EMC (inside JJR's A$4,000–7,000 "small household appliance" range), plus **A$5,000** RF for Wi-Fi (low-middle of A$3,500–15,000, assuming a pre-tested Wi-Fi module's reports partly carry over).
  - **"B+"**: add A$3,300 if the adapter also needs its own AU certificate (A$800 SAA fee + ≈A$2,500 testing, assumption).
  - Lead time 3–6 weeks for testing (assumption).
- **Cyber is a gating item for Wi-Fi units,** not a cost item. Units manufactured on or after 4 Mar 2026 need the factory to write a statement of compliance naming a defined support period, a published vulnerability-reporting channel and no universal default passwords. For a white-label Tuya-type product, the importer that puts its brand on the box may itself be the "manufacturer" for these purposes (my inference from the ACL s.7(1)(c) analogy; not verified for the Cyber Security Act). In that case the importer must obtain and stand behind the evidence.
- **Claims discipline:** sell "H13" only with an EN 1822/ISO 29463 filter test report for the actual filter. Sell "CADR xxx m³/h" only with a chamber test report (AHAM AC-1, GB/T 18801 or IEC 63086-type method). Express room size as the test-method calculation, not "up to 150 m²". Avoid ozone/ioniser marketing and any virus, COVID or asthma-treatment claim: TGA risk, and CHOICE advises avoiding ozone models.

### Gaps
- No regulator or certifier statement naming the EESS level of "air purifiers" was found. The Level 1 conclusion rests on the definitions and the default rule; ask a JAS-ANZ certifier to confirm in writing.
- The ACMA EMC compliance level for a purifier with a brushless DC motor, MCU and SMPS was not read from the 2017 Labelling Notice (acma.gov.au unreachable through robots). Treating it as level 2 (test report required) is an assumption.

## Q5. Unit economics on the shared base (two tiers, 200 units and full container, Scenarios A/B)

### Takeaway
At the brief's **200-unit first order**, purifiers make at most a thin margin, and only when the factory's existing test reports can be used (Scenario A): **15–21% GP (A$11–20/unit) on cheap generic sources**. Examples: Konwin CADR-250 at A$99 is 17.6%; with Wi-Fi at A$109 it is 20.6%; Yinyu small-room at A$79 is 15.5%. Every row turns **loss-making once Australian testing must be paid for** (Scenario B: −17% to −92%). The in-band CADR-320–350 Wi-Fi units (A$119–129) lose money at 200 units. Even at 1,000–1,700 units, Kuiguan earns only 6–18%, and the Foshan trader Purecare still loses money at 910 units. Margins become healthy only at MOQ/container scale: **Konwin 1,000 units in one 20GP earns 27–34% at A$99 (cash ≈A$52–58k), and 24–36% with Wi-Fi at A$109 (A$56–67k)**. But that means selling 1,000+ no-name units against A$89–129 Amazon alternatives. Break-even FOB at 200 units/A$99 is US$43.5 (A) or US$22.7 (B), against a listed US$30–33.

**Formula** (identical to verify_camping_appliance.md, so results are comparable):
- Landed/unit = FOB × 1.4376 × (1 + duty) + per-unit compliance (US$1 for RCM labels, AU plug and English manual; assumption) + freight ÷ units + A$400 ÷ units + fixed compliance ÷ units

## Q6. Verdict and what would have to be true

### Takeaway
**Score: 3/10. Not a go for the brief as written** (200 units, A$100–400, Marketplace). The reason is not compliance: a mains-corded purifier is EESS Level 1, one of the lightest regimes for any mains appliance. The reasons are:
1. The Amazon/Kmart price floor forces no-name units to A$79–119, at or below the bottom of the band.
2. There is no bulky-goods pickup edge.
3. A 200-unit order earns ≤21% only if the factory's test reports are usable, and loses money if testing must be bought.
4. Buyers have little reason to trust an unknown brand's health claims over a A$99–129 Levoit or Philips with free next-day delivery.

It becomes a **conditional 4/10** at MOQ scale (1,000 units, one 20GP, ≈A$52–58k cash, 27–34% GP) if the conditions below hold.

### Inferences
**Scoring reasoning (same 1–10 scale as vertical_appliances_emobility.md):**
- **+ compliance:** Level 1, no certificate, no equipment registration; RS A$239.74/yr; claims evidence obtainable from factory reports. This is a genuine advantage over air fryers, heaters and espresso machines, which are Level 3.
- **+ freight and cash at small volume:** cartons of 0.02–0.03 m³ mean A$11/unit LCL and A$6/unit in a 20GP.
- **+ demand:** recurring spring demand and occasional bushfire-smoke sell-outs.
- **− price band:** small units must sell at A$69–79 (out of band). Medium no-name units sell at A$99–119, right where Philips (A$129), Levoit (A$99–169) and Kmart (A$149) sit with brand trust, apps, returns and free delivery.
- **− channel:** no pickup advantage (Australia Post-sized cartons). The health-claim purchase favours reviewed brands. The ozone/ioniser and fire-recall history (CPSC Aroeve, 191k units) raises trust and liability stakes for no-name units. The importer is the ACL "manufacturer" (s.7(1)(e)).
- **− volume:** good margins need 800–1,000+ units of one model. There is no evidence that Marketplace can absorb that, and stock risk is high in a smoke-free summer.

**What would have to be true for it to work (all of these together):**
1. **A mains-corded Level 1 model** (internal PSU, no heater, humidifier or fan-for-cooling function). Otherwise the adapter must carry an RCM Certificate of Conformity whose holder authorises you in writing, and you must register it (A$89.85/yr).
2. **The factory hands over full English test reports** for the exact model: IEC/AS-NZS 60335-2-65 + AU differences, CISPR 14-1 EMC, AS/NZS 4268 for Wi-Fi, an EN 1822 filter-class report for "H13" and a chamber CADR report. That is Scenario A. For Wi-Fi units made on or after 4 Mar 2026 it must also supply a signed cyber statement of compliance naming a support period.
3. **A price tier Kmart and Philips do not occupy:** CADR ≥250 m³/h at **A$99 without app** or **A$109 with Wi-Fi**, below the Philips 600i (A$129, CADR 170) and Kmart Anko Large (A$149). Verified CADR and real 30–40 m² coverage are the selling point; no ozone, ioniser or virus claims.
4. **MOQ-scale buying with a supplier like Konwin:** about 1,000 units in one 20GP, ≈A$52–58k cash plus ≈A$5k import GST, landed by **mid-September** for the spring allergy season and the bushfire summer.
5. **Evidence that Marketplace can move ≥15–20 units/week** at A$99–109 against Amazon. This is unknown and should be tested first, for example with a 50–100-unit pilot from a low-MOQ supplier, accepting a loss on that pilot.

If any of 1, 2 or 4 fails, the economics fall back to the 200-unit Scenario B rows (losses). That is the practical answer to "why is a home air filter not a go?"

### Gaps
- The score rests on modelled margins with assumed testing costs and unverified CADR/H13 claims. It also rests on no measured Marketplace demand, and on no written certifier confirmation of the Level 1 classification.
