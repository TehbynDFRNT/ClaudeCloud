# Australian product value chains, layer by layer: what each layer earns, how trade distribution works, which lean challengers took a layer, which barriers protect the layers, and a scoring rubric for new categories

*Research date: 2026-10-10. AUD unless stated. Status: written incrementally; this file is the framework and layer-margin evidence. Sibling researchers cover specific categories.*

*Conventions:*
- *"Agg." means figures from the stockanalysis.com income-statement pages (an aggregator that restates company accounts; fetched 2026-10-10). Primary filings are cited where I downloaded and read them.*
- *Price ÷ COGS = 1 ÷ (1 − gross margin). This is the multiple between what the company sells for and what the goods cost it. My arithmetic, not company-reported.*
- *"Inference" means my reasoning, not a sourced fact.*
- *FX for worked examples: A$1 = US$0.65. This is an assumption, not a sourced spot rate. For reference, Reece's FY26 constant-currency restatement used US$0.6471 ([Reece FY26 results](https://announcements.asx.com.au/asxpdf/20260824/pdf/073339wfnfjcqp.pdf)).*

## Q1. Layer margins from public filings (FY2025/FY2026): what each listed brand owner, distributor and retailer earns

### Takeaway
FY26 filings show four margin bands, one per layer.
- **Pure trade distributors** of other people's brands earn about **25–32% gross margin**, roughly 1.35–1.45× their buy price. They keep **3–9% EBIT**. Examples: Reece 28.9%; Rexel, Bunzl, Ferguson, Watsco, Pool Corp and Beijer Ref 25–32%.
- **Brand owners selling into those channels** earn about **35–50% gross margin**, roughly 1.5–2.0× landed or manufactured cost. They keep **10–19% EBIT**. Examples: GWA 40.6%, RWC 35.2%, Breville 36.0%, Waterco 50.4%.
- **Firms owning design, import and their own retail or trade counter** earn **62–83% gross margin**, roughly 2.7–5.8× landed cost. Examples: Beacon Lighting 68.6%, Nick Scali 65.6%, Dusk 64.6%, Lovisa 82.6%.
- **Retailers of third-party brands** sit at about **22–31%**. Examples: JB Hi-Fi 22.4%, Temple & Webster 30.2% delivered margin.

The "thick" layer for an importer is therefore the **brand owner + importer** layer. When that layer also owns the customer channel (stores, a trade program, an install crew), the vertically integrated players show it can be worth a further 1.5–2× on top of a distributor's cut.

### Inferences
- **Converting margin to a price ladder (inference).** Gross margin is measured on *landed* cost, not FOB: FOB plus freight, insurance, duty, clearance and inbound logistics, and for manufacturers, factory cost. Layer multiples on cost:
  - pure distributor ≈ 1.35–1.47×;
  - brand owner selling to trade/retail ≈ 1.5–2.0×;
  - vertically integrated brand + own retail/trade counter ≈ 2.7–3.2× for lighting/furniture/candles, and 5.75× at the extreme (Lovisa).

  Stacked for a plumbing-type product (brand owner 1.6–1.8× → merchant 1.35–1.45× → installer materials markup → +10% GST), the consumer pays about **2.4–3.2× landed cost on the goods alone**, before installer markup and labour. With landed cost at maybe 1.15–1.3× FOB (assumption; landed cost is a sibling topic), that is about **2.8–4.2× FOB** for supply-only through a conventional chain.
- **The water-filter case sits at or above the top of a normal stacked chain (inference).**
  - US$290 FOB ≈ A$446 at the assumed 0.65 rate.
  - Supply-only A$1,300–1,500 is 2.9–3.4× FOB in AUD.
  - Installed A$2,200–3,000 is 4.9–6.7×.
  - A private-label seller that holds the WaterMark-listed brand and sells through its own install channel is collapsing three layers (importer/brand, merchant, installer) into one. That is the Beacon/Nick Scali structure applied to plumbing.
- **What is capturable is the bypassed layer's gross margin minus the costs it really incurs (inference).**
  - Reece keeps ~8.6% EBIT in ANZ on ~29% gross margin. So ~20 points of a merchant's margin pays for branches, stock, credit, delivery and staff, which a challenger must replace or avoid.
  - Brand owners keep much more (GWA 18.5% EBIT on ~40.6% GM). The brand/importer layer is the thicker *profit* pool, not just the thicker *gross* pool.
  - Blackwoods (4.3% EBT) and Metcash Hardware & Tools (~5% EBIT) show that broad-range MRO/hardware distribution is a thin-profit, scale-dependent layer. It is a poor target for a small operator.
- **Vertically integrated brand + channel is where margins are highest (inference).**
  - Beacon (68.6% GM, 43% trade) shows that owning the brand *and* the trade relationship lets a firm skip the wholesaler while keeping retail-level gross margin.
  - Nick Scali and Lovisa show the same for retail.
  - The cost is high opex: Beacon's operating expenses are 44% of sales, so the EBIT margin is 14%, not 68%.
- **Basis caveats (inference).**
  - ARB reports expenses by nature, so an aggregator "COGS" may exclude manufacturing labour and overhead. That is likely why Eulerpool's 32.7% and the agg. 56.8% differ. Use ARB's EBIT margin (16.7%) for comparison.
  - Kogan's gross margin includes commission-based marketplace and membership revenue.
  - Harvey Norman's company revenue excludes A$6.58bn of franchisee sales.
  - Wesfarmers does not disclose divisional gross margins.
- **Kmart (9.4% EBT on A$11.8bn) is the scale proof (inference).** A retailer that internalises the brand and importer layers (direct-sourced own brand) out-earns a retailer of third-party brands (JB Hi-Fi 6.6% EBIT at 22.4% GM).

### Gaps
- Reece and GWA do not show gross margin in the results documents I read. Their figures above are aggregator-derived (cost of sales from the statutory accounts). Reece's and GWA's FY26 annual reports were not downloaded.
- No public gross or EBIT margins were found for the private wholesalers: Tradelink, Plumbers Supplies Co-op, Rexel Australia, the Sonepar businesses (L&H/CNW status not verified), MMEM, Actrol (Reece-owned, not separately reported), Heatcraft, RSEA, Bunzl Australasia.
- Wesfarmers does not disclose Bunnings' or Blackwoods' gross margin.
- No company discloses its FOB-to-landed ratio. All FOB-based multiples above are inferences.

## Q2. How trade distribution works in Australia (plumbing, electrical, HVAC-R, building, industrial/safety): margins, trade pricing, credit, rebates, services, and what a brand needs to get ranged

### Takeaway
Trade distribution is a dense branch-network business: about **678 Reece ANZ branches** (Actrol, a Reece company, has 80+ HVAC-R branches), **200+ Tradelink branches**, plus electrical and industrial networks.
- **Economics:** about **25–32% gross margin** and only **4–9% EBIT**. Reece ANZ makes 8.6% EBIT; Blackwoods 4.3% EBT.
- **What the merchant sells:** working capital, logistics and risk transfer, not the product. That means:
  - negotiated trade prices shown against RRP;
  - 30-day credit with retention of title;
  - same-day branch stock and delivery to site;
  - a single point for warranty and returns;
  - specification help for architects;
  - showrooms where plumbers send their clients.
- **Who funds it:** brand owners, through supplier volume rebates and promotional funding (Metcash, RWC). Merchants run their own trade-loyalty rebates on top, e.g. Bunnings PowerPass Pro ≈ 5% back in rewards dollars, and the Beacon Cash rebate.
- **What a brand needs to get ranged** (from the cited evidence): mandatory certification held in the brand's or licensee's name; national warranty and recall capacity; delivery reliability (GWA targets DIFOT >90%); plumber-facing technical/rep coverage; a rebate and promotion budget. Exclusives for a key merchant help win shelf space.
- **What the installer adds on top:** a materials markup that, in the sources found, is about 20–30%.

### Inferences
- **The stacked price, worked through (inference).** Take a WaterMark product that a brand owner sells to Reece at 100.
  - A ~29% merchant gross margin implies a plumber trade price of about 140 ex GST.
  - A 20–30% plumber materials markup gives about 168–182 ex GST, or about 185–200 inc GST, before labour.
  - If the brand owner's own ~40% gross margin (GWA) sits underneath, its landed cost is about 60. The consumer then pays roughly **3.1–3.3× landed cost** for the product component. That matches Q1.
  - A lean operator that is both brand/licensee and installer collects the brand margin (~40 points), the merchant margin (~29 points) and the installer materials markup in one hand.
- **Why installers still use merchants (inference from the terms above).**
  - 30-day credit funds the job until the customer pays.
  - Branch density gives same-day replacement parts.
  - Merchants hold compliant, WaterMark-listed stock, so the plumber is not exposed for installing non-compliant product (see Q4).
  - The merchant is a single counter for warranty claims and returns.
  - Showrooms do the client-selection work.

  A challenger selling direct to plumbers must replace these: offer trade credit, fast local stock or overnight dispatch, a simple warranty-swap process and spec sheets. Otherwise it must sell to the end customer with its own installers, as CHF, WAA and Pure Water Systems do (Q3).
- **Getting ranged costs more than certification (inference).**
  - Evidence of what merchants expect: rebates (Metcash), exclusive ranges and plumber engagement (GWA), delivery KPIs (DIFOT >90%), and price-change-notification cycles (Tradelink and Middy's pages).
  - A newcomer would also likely need a rebate/promo budget of a few % of sales, national stock, rep coverage and enough balance sheet to carry merchant payment terms.
  - Hence, for a small operator, **direct-to-installer or direct-to-consumer with own install** is usually more clearable than getting ranged at Reece, Tradelink or Bunnings.
- **The merchant layer is thin-profit, so it rarely retaliates through pricing (inference).** It does, however, control shelf access and spec influence. An incumbent brand's main lever against a challenger is merchant exclusivity and rebates, not consumer price. This bears on "incumbent reaction risk" in Q5.
