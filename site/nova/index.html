Nova, Episode 1: David & Goliath

a film by Tehbyn Nova

A white dwarf no bigger than Earth feeds on a red giant until the stolen hydrogen ignites, and from Earth a new star appears.

Watch 2 min 48 s · vertical 9:16 · rendered entirely in code

The story

Goliath
a red giant
David
a white dwarf

Two stars circle each other in the dark. Goliath is a red giant, a boiling mass of crimson gas with convection cells the size of suns. David is a white dwarf: no bigger than Earth, far denser, a cold blue-white point.

The giant has swollen until its outer layers reach the inner Lagrange point, where the pull of the two stars balances. Past that point the dwarf’s gravity wins. Matter tears loose in a stream of fire, curves across the gap and spirals into a vortex around the dwarf. It settles on the dwarf’s surface as an ocean of hydrogen, packed hotter and tighter with every turn. Five strikes land in the orchestra, and each one takes light from the picture.

Then the ocean ignites, and you see nothing. The screen is black; the climax is a sound, a pressure that keeps rising. When the eruption breaks out of the dark, the explosion lands on that same frame, and Vivaldi’s closing tutti comes back under cannon salvos. The shell fractures into gold. The giant is left scarred, undone by its own substance. The small core endures, as the white dwarf of a real nova does, and it can erupt again.

The view moves to a dark-sky Earth. Under the Milky Way, a point of light appears where there was none: a new star, a nova.

Last, the marble David, who has been turning toward the lens all film, looks straight at you.

The serieswhat Nova means

Nova retells old stories as events that really happen in the sky. In Episode 1 the shepherd and the giant become a white dwarf and a red giant, and the theft of fire is in there too, because the physics fits the myth: the small, dense and determined against the immense.

The name sets the terms. Nova is Latin for new. Observers saw a star where none had been and called it new, though what they were seeing was an explosion. The series keeps both readings at once: destruction that is also a birth, and a survivor that can erupt again.

Between the stars are the notebooks: iron-gall ink and red chalk, mirror script, studies drawn in the manner of Leonardo. The figure-eight of two gravities, the stolen fire, the deluge, the sling, the CNO cycle, the rings. They stand for the oldest part of the story, people trying to draw what they could not reach.

And there is the figure who watches. The marble David appears in brief inserts on black, always framed the same way. Across the whole film he turns ninety degrees, from profile toward the lens, so slowly that each glimpse looks still. In the last shot he is looking at you.

How it was made

Nothing in Nova was filmed. Every frame is computed: a WebGL engine renders light in high dynamic range and is stepped one frame at a time inside headless Chromium, 4,025 frames at 1080 by 1920 and 24 frames per second. The sound design is synthesised and the whole soundtrack is mixed in code. The marble head is real: a public-domain 3D scan of a plaster cast of Michelangelo’s David. The notebook studies are drawn in code in the manner of Leonardo, not by him. The score is an edit of Vivaldi’s Winter, bars 1 to 38 and then bars 56 to 63, with cannon fire from Tchaikovsky’s 1812 Overture placed first as approaching guns, then as salvos.
