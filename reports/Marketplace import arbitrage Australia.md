# Bulky, Boring Imports Beat Australia's Retail Floors

Three bulky, non-electrical products survive a full landed-cost check against Australia's real price floors. A **14 ft trampoline with enclosure** earns about **A$97 gross profit per unit (31%) on a ~A$28k, 128-unit 20ft container**. A **100 kg rubber bumper-plate set** earns about **A$79 per set (26%) on a ~A$47k weight-capped 20ft**, or A$58 on a A$12k trial of 50 sets. A **double hammock with steel stand** earns about **A$29–32 per unit (33–36%) on a ~A$11k, 200-unit LCL order**. Dual-motor standing desks, timber cubbies and aluminium camp kitchens earn 25–44%, but only at full-container scale and with A$27–101k of cash. Mattresses, collapsible egg chairs, 4WD recovery kits, 40 kg dial dumbbells and pressure washers are marginal. Most of the other categories most likely to sell fast on Marketplace are not viable: kids' ride-ons, wicker lounge sets, gas-lift beds, walking pads, spin bikes and 12V fridges. Australian importer brands, Kmart, Aldi, Bunnings and Super Retail Group house brands already sell them at or below a small importer's landed cost, and that floor is often well below Amazon AU. Nobody measures Marketplace sell-through. Meta publishes nothing and listings sit behind a login, so the demand ranking rests on seller guidance, asking prices and Australia Post's 22 kg parcel limit. In every scenario the binding risk is selling 100–1,100 bulky units by local pickup, not the margin. Real factories exist for each winner: Zhejiang Lingsha (Funjump) for trampolines, Nantong Tengtai and Nantong Ironman for plates, Zhejiang Prorials and Jiecang for desk frames. But the cheapest listing is often a trading company, and no platform badge proves factory status. The cheapest way to verify a supplier is nearly free: Made-in-China.com for supplier-level data, China's official GSXT registry for business scope and insured headcount, and ImportYeti's free tier for US brand customers, followed by a ~US$250-a-day audit before any deposit. All margins are modelled gross profit. They use listed (not quoted) factory prices and estimated compliance costs, and they come before the seller's time, insurance and extended storage.

## Nobody measures Marketplace speed, but parcel limits pick the categories

Meta publishes no Australian Marketplace category or sales data, and Marketplace search requires a login. No source reports sold prices or days-to-sell for brand-new goods in the A$100–400 band. The only Australian time-to-sale figure is an unsourced seller guide's claim that "priced right, furniture sells within 24–48 hours". The same guide names furniture, kids' gear, gym equipment, outdoor and garden goods, and small appliances as the strongest categories, and says gym gear sells best "in January and after winter" ([Roopon](https://www.roopon.com/blog/how-to-sell-on-facebook-marketplace-australia)). Hard category data points the same way. Household goods posted the largest retail gain in November 2025, at **+10.1% year on year** ([ARA](https://www.retail.org.au/media/early-christmas-shopping-drives-strong-november-retail-growth)). Home & Garden was Australia's third-largest online category in 2025, at **A$11.4bn** ([Retail World](https://retailworldmagazine.com.au/australia-post-reveals-aussies-spent-82-6-billion-online/)). Any claim that one product is Marketplace's "fastest seller" is therefore a judgement built from proxies, not a measurement.

The proxy that does the most work is parcel size. Australia Post caps a domestic parcel at **22 kg, 105 cm and 0.25 m³** ([Australia Post](https://auspost.com.au/content/dam/auspost_corp/media/documents/post-guides/post-charges-guide-ms11.pdf)). Trampolines, heavy dumbbells, racks, mattresses, lounge sets and fridges therefore travel by courier, at extra cost and with delay. Online importer brands charge postcode-based delivery on almost every bulky item, from about A$45 for a gazebo to A$100–130 for treadmills and large trampolines. Kmart charged **A$101.97** to deliver one Gardeon lounge set ([BuyWisely](https://buywisely.com.au/product/gardeon-outdoor-furniture-sofa-set-4-seater-wicker-lounge-setting-table)). A seller holding local stock can offer same-day pickup and inspection before payment. That matters on a platform the ABC says has "a reputation for being overrun with scammers" ([ABC](https://www.abc.net.au/news/science/2024-03-01/facebook-marketplace-has-become-the-home-of-scammers/103521536)). Small, parcel-friendly goods get no such edge. They compete head-on with Amazon Prime and with Temu, which had **5 million Australian shoppers** in 2025 ([Roy Morgan](https://www.roymorgan.com/findings/10158-amazon-temu-and-shein-growth-story-continues-march-2026)).

The categories with the strongest demand signals are also the most crowded. Dropshipzone, a B2B drop-ship marketplace with more than 100,000 SKUs ([PackSend](https://www.packsend.com.au/blog/top-dropshipping-suppliers/)), carries house brands such as Gardeon, Artiss, Everfit and Rigo ([Dropshipzone](https://www.dropshipzone.com.au/)). Their listings repeat from city to city at identical prices. A capped search sample of Sydney classifieds, not a market count, turned up **15 brand-new-labelled adjustable-dumbbell listings, 12 rack listings and 11 spin-bike listings**, and every category showed signs of repeat sellers ([Gumtree](https://www.gumtree.com.au/s-gym-fitness/sydney/adjustable+dumbbells/k0c18565l3003435)). Much of this saturation is virtual. Drop-shippers with no local stock set a visible price anchor but cannot offer pickup, so an importer that holds stock can still win on speed and trust if it prices near that anchor. Timing matters as much as category. The November Cyber Monday and Black Friday period drives the year's biggest household-goods spike ([ABS](https://www.abs.gov.au/media-centre/media-releases/retail-spending-steady-december)). January is the gym window, September to December is outdoor season, and kids' gift lines peak from late October to Christmas.

## Landed cost runs near 1.5× FOB, and Amazon is rarely the real ceiling

Every margin in this report comes from one cost model, re-run on retail prices observed on 9 October 2026, so products can be compared directly. The model converts at **US$1 = A$1.4376** ([RBA](https://www.rba.gov.au/statistics/tables/csv/f11.1-data.csv)). It assumes door-delivered freight of A$10,064 per 40ft high-cube container, A$5,908 per 20ft, and A$244 per weight-or-measure tonne plus A$900 per shipment for LCL (less-than-container loads). Those all-in figures combine forwarder ocean quotes of US$1,500–1,800 per 20ft and US$2,900–3,600 per 40ft ([ExFreight](https://www.exfreight.com/shipping-from-china-to-australia/)), carrier increases of about US$1,000 per TEU in September ([KLN](https://info.oceania.kln.com/customer-advisory/fcl-shipping-rates-from-china-to-australia-september-2026-outlook)) and Australian destination charges such as Maersk's A$600 Sydney terminal fee per 20ft ([Maersk](https://www.maersk.com/news/articles/2026/09/01/maersk-terminal-handling-service-australia)). They are modelling assumptions, not freight quotes. Duty is **0% under the China–Australia FTA (ChAFTA), but only if a valid Certificate of Origin is held at import** ([DFAT](https://www.dfat.gov.au/trade/agreements/in-force/chafta/doing-business-with-china/chafta-certificates-of-origin); [ABF](https://www.abf.gov.au/help-and-support-subsite/CustomsNotices/2026-24.pdf)). Without one, most of these lines pay 5%. Import GST is 10% of customs value plus duty and freight, and a GST-registered importer recovers it ([ABF](https://www.abf.gov.au/importing-exporting-and-manufacturing/importing/cost-of-importing-goods/gst-and-other-taxes)). Revenue is therefore counted net of GST, as sale price ÷ 1.1. Each entry also pays an A$50–152 processing charge ([ABF](https://www.abf.gov.au/importing-exporting-and-manufacturing/importing/cost-of-importing-goods/charges/import-processing-charge)) and A$71 for biosecurity ([ABF](https://www.abf.gov.au/help-and-support-subsite/CustomsNotices/2026-23.pdf)).

On top of that come a A$400 pre-shipment inspection per factory (the market rate is US$200–350 per man-day; [Supplyia](https://www.supplyia.com/inspection-company-in-china/)), a 3% defect allowance (5% for electricals), handling or storage, and product-specific testing. Facebook charges no fee on local-pickup sales ([SaleHoo](https://www.salehoo.com/learn/facebook-marketplace-dropshipping)). A worked example on 2026 rate cards puts 100 units at US$60 FOB, shipped LCL to Sydney, at about **A$130 per unit landed, roughly 1.5× the FOB price in Australian dollars** (range 1.3–1.8×). Gross profit is net revenue minus landed cost. The cash figures exclude the import GST, which the importer pays up front and recovers later.

Two physical limits decide which products survive. Iron is limited by weight. A 20ft box is capped at 21.8–24.3 tonnes depending on the state ([ICE Cargo](https://icecargo.com.au/transport-options/)). A box of bumper plates therefore fills at about 211 sets while using only 21 of its 28 cubic metres. A 40ft box carries no more weight, which makes it the wrong container for iron. Furniture is limited by volume: a full-size recliner takes a third of a cubic metre, so its share of a 20ft box alone costs A$70. Order size is the other big lever. Across the shortlist, gross profit per unit typically rises by 40% to fourfold between a first LCL order and a full container. That is why most viable plays need A$20–100k of committed cash.

The re-verification's most important finding is that **Amazon AU is rarely the cheapest comparable new item**. Earlier category work that anchored on Amazon overstated margins as a result. Super Retail Group's house brands sell at "Club" prices to anyone who signs up for free. They set the floor at **A$119.99** for a 7-piece XTM recovery kit ([BCF](https://www.bcf.com.au/p/xtm-7-piece-recovery-kit/600254.html)), **A$99** on clearance for a Wanderer dual-cupboard camp kitchen ([Supercheap Auto](https://www.supercheapauto.com.au/search?q=camp%20kitchen)) and **A$89.99** for a ToolPRO pressure washer ([Supercheap Auto](https://www.supercheapauto.com.au/search?q=pressure%20washer&srule=price-low-to-high)). Aldi's Ferrex 1600W washer goes on sale at A$89.99 on 17 October ([Aldi](https://www.aldi.com.au/product/ferrex-high-pressure-washer-1600w-000000000000739767)). Kmart lists a 12ft springless trampoline at A$299 ([Kmart](https://www.kmart.com.au/product/12-foot-springless-trampoline-42836155/)). Costway sells its hammock with stand direct at A$105.95 ([PriceHipster](https://pricehipster.com/product/AZ5tnapOcACzYldIydzs9A)). These walk-in alternatives come with returns and Click & Collect, so a no-name Marketplace listing has to undercut them, not just Amazon. As a result, camp kitchens, pressure washers, dog crates and kettlebell sets, which looked like A$110–139 items, become A$85–99 items, just below the A$100 band. The lower half of the band is exactly where mass-retail house brands are densest.

The **14 ft trampoline** is the strongest opportunity because demand evidence, pickup advantage and margin all line up. Zhejiang Lingsha lists a 14 ft-specific price of **US$96** and a single carton of 0.2176 cubic metres and 62 kg ([Made-in-China](https://lsfunjump.en.made-in-china.com/product/uphRrGWYIgci/China-Funjump-14FT-Outdoor-Indoor-Fitness-Kid-Child-Mini-Bungee-Jumping-Trampoline-with-Safety-Enclosure.html)), better than the US$110 originally estimated. Amazon's only 14 ft offer starts at A$385.95 before delivery, and delivery for a comparable 16 ft model ran about **A$120 to metro Brisbane** ([OzBargain](https://www.ozbargain.com.au/node/965103)). At A$349 a 128-unit 20GP earns about A$97 a unit and breaks even at A$242, so the margin survives a Black Friday cut to A$329 (A$77, 26%). Pricing at A$339 keeps it 12% under Amazon on the item price alone. The pressure point is the smaller backyard. Kmart's 12ft springless model is only A$50 cheaper, so listings should lead with the 14 ft's 150 kg rating and roughly 36% more mat area. Trampolines have only a voluntary standard, AS 4989 ([Standards Australia](https://store.standards.org.au/product/as-4989-2015)). Still, a test of the exact build (about A$2,500, estimated) and product-liability cover are prudent for an injury-prone children's product.

**Bumper plates** have the most robust margin on the list, because every input verified. The US$1.00–1.50 per kg band holds across eight-plus Made-in-China listings, with manufacturer Nantong Tengtai at **US$1.00/kg** for a one-tonne minimum order ([Made-in-China](https://tt-sports.en.made-in-china.com/product/mQlUwAIbaHVq/China-Gym-Fitness-Bumper-Plate-Rubber-Competition-Weight-Bumper-Plate.html)). At a mid-case US$1.30/kg, a weight-capped 20GP earns A$79 a set. That rises to A$124 (40%) at Tengtai's price and holds at A$50 (16%) even at US$1.50. The A$339 target is 15% under BRIXX's A$400 Amazon price, which appeared only in search results, and 27% under the verified CORTEX set. No mandatory Australian standard covers free weights. The risks are on the selling side: 100 kg sets are hard for buyers to collect, used plates hold their value, and the container pays back only after about 152 of 211 sets sell. One weight-limited box of 150 bumper sets and 70 pairs of 2×40 kg dial dumbbells returns about **A$15.8k gross profit on A$53.0k cash** (22%) from just two factories. Production runs 15–45 days and transit 23–31 days ([KLN](https://info.oceania.kln.com/customer-advisory/fcl-shipping-rates-from-china-to-australia-september-2026-outlook)), so iron ordered in October can plausibly land for January's gym peak.

The **double hammock with steel stand** is the cheapest way in. The carton is small (0.061 cubic metres, 11 kg) and the listed price is US$20–21 at a 200-unit minimum ([Made-in-China](https://lounger2.en.made-in-china.com/product/zatrBMfGjmRi/China-Easy-Assemble-Folding-Double-Portable-Hammock-with-Stand.html)). A 200-unit LCL therefore lands at about A$58 a unit and earns A$29 at A$95, the best first-order percentage margin of any product checked, on A$10.8k of cash. At A$99 it sits inside the band and still undercuts Costway and Amazon. In a modelled mixed 40HQ of hammocks, egg chairs and pizza ovens, hammocks produced 48% of the gross profit on 23% of the cash. The weaknesses are real. Absolute profit per sale is small for a time-heavy pickup channel, and the cheapest supplier is a Ningbo trading company. Crazy Sales priced a comparable set at A$84.95–89.95 for weeks in mid-2026 ([PriceHipster](https://pricehipster.com/product/AZ4bQp-9cASueipXtq0y6w)). And an 11 kg carton is cheap for online rivals to courier.

The next tier works only at container scale. A **dual-motor standing desk** at A$299 is well under Amazon's cheapest complete dual-motor desk. But it is only 6.5% under Advwin's A$319.90 direct price ([Advwin](https://www.advwin.com.au/products/advwin-dual-motor-electric-standing-desk-height-adjustable)) and competes with single-motor 140×70 cm desks at A$199.95 delivered ([Amazon AU](https://www.amazon.com.au/dp/B0CXHYLVZM)). It makes A$47 a desk on a 100-unit first order and A$84–95 on 274–571 desks. The electrical-compliance bill (A$1.7–6.3k, unverified) swings first-order profit by roughly A$15–30 a desk. A **timber cubby** at A$299 earns A$73 on a 200-unit 20GP from Ningbo Dreamgo, against Kmart Marketplace cubbies at A$349.97–399 ([Kmart](https://www.kmart.com.au/product/ausway-outdoor-kids-cubby-house-wooden-playhouse-110154373/)). But no Amazon floor could be observed, and Kmart's own Activo cubby carries an RRP of A$169 ([Kmart](https://www.kmart.com.au/product/activo-lets-go-play-wooden-cubby-house-with-kitchen-43271481/)). Solid fir also needs an offshore treatment certificate to clear biosecurity. An **aluminium camp kitchen** earns A$28 (31.5%) at A$99 only with Welfull's 500-unit minimum in a full 20GP. The 100-unit fallback loses money because the trading company offering it charges US$41 for a bulkier carton.

The marginal tier makes money only with a better-than-listed quote or a full container. **Queen mattresses** clear just A$3 a unit on a 100-unit first order. That rises to A$21–34 at container scale, or A$19–50 if Foshan Aussie Hcl's US$48 listing holds for a comparable spec. Even then, it is the most saturated category on Marketplace, and an unboxed mattress cannot be resold. **Collapsible egg chairs** earn A$11 on a first LCL, because Sydney Marketplace already shows "brand new" chairs at about A$200 and one at A$90 clearance ([Facebook](https://www.facebook.com/marketplace/sydney/egg-chairs/)). The **4WD recovery kit** loses A$11 a kit on 100 units once its fixed costs are spread. Those costs are about A$4k (estimated) for the mandatory snatch-strap labelling regime ([Federal Register of Legislation](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2017L01560/latest/text)), independent break tests and liability cover. It needs a 1,120-kit, A$63k container to reach 41%. **2×40 kg dial dumbbells** make A$57 (17%) a pair in a 215-pair box, but their Amazon comparable has a single rating, so demand is unproven. The **2000W pressure washer** is EESS Level 1, not Level 3, so it needs no Australian certificate ([EESS](https://www.eess.gov.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/EESS-Inscope-Equipment-Definitions-and-Risk-Levels-v4.2-.pdf)). But Bunnings, Aldi and Supercheap sell branded units at A$89.99–99 with instant returns, so its 13–25% margin depends on a 500-unit order and on reusing the factory's test reports.

Three more candidates looked positive in first-pass category work but were not re-verified: a 230V-capable diesel heater (about 18% at A$149 on 200 units, for winter 2027), a heavy-duty 3×3 m gazebo with walls (about 24% at A$179 against Kings' A$199–349 Commercial model; [4WD Supacentre](https://www.4wdsupacentre.com.au/camping/gazebos.html)) and a steel fire-pit table (18% only at container scale). Re-verification cut most first-pass margins, so treat these as leads, not opportunities. Timing also limits what is reachable this season. Purchase order to sellable stock takes roughly 10–13 weeks ([KLN](https://info.oceania.kln.com/customer-advisory/fcl-shipping-rates-from-china-to-australia-september-2026-outlook)), and Chinese New Year falls on 6 February 2027. A mid-October order for outdoor goods lands around January, after Black Friday and Christmas. The realistic windows are January for plates, spring–summer 2027 for hammocks and camp kitchens, and Christmas 2027 for trampolines and cubbies, ordered in July–August. Several inputs remain open: no supplier was sent a request for quotation, several carton sizes are estimates, Amazon delivery fees were hidden, and Kmart and Bunnings prices come from undated search snippets.

## Most fast sellers fail on price floors, cube or compliance

Candidates fail for one of three reasons. The most common is a price floor at or below a small importer's landed cost. The floor sellers (Artiss, Everfit, Gardeon, Kings, BABY JOY, Costway, Kmart's Anko) are themselves container-scale China importers with Australian warehouses. The second is cube: bulky, low-value goods spend more on freight than their margin can absorb. The third is compliance: mains appliances that need a per-model Australian certificate, e-mobility under new NSW rules, gas appliances that need Australian certification, and children's or toppling products with mandatory warnings.

Compliance also shapes the viable products. An importer of goods whose maker has no Australian presence is the Australian Consumer Law's "deemed manufacturer". It must provide refunds and repairs itself and carries injury liability ([LegalVision](https://legalvision.com.au/legal-considerations-of-importers-under-australian-consumer-law)). That is the strongest reason to stay out of batteries, nursery goods and gas, where one claim can exceed years of margin. The channel itself carries risk too. Meta's Commerce Policies state that Marketplace "is intended for consumer-to-consumer sales" and already let Meta suspend business sellers in the EEA, the Philippines and India ([Meta](https://www.facebook.com/policies_center/commerce/)). A business built on Marketplace is building on a platform that tolerates it rather than courts it. The only detailed Australian first-hand account found, an April 2026 OzBargain post, described **more than A$6,000 in losses** from damage, uncertified stock and a Foshan supplier that never shipped. The poster's conclusion: competing on price against cheap imports was not viable ([OzBargain](https://www.ozbargain.com.au/node/954147)).

The cheapest listing is often a trader, and the labels are thin. On one Made-in-China results page for adjustable dumbbells, **only 3 of 30 cards carried a Manufacturer/Factory label, and all three also said Trading Company**. Thirteen were labelled Trading Company, yet 28 showed an "Audited Supplier" badge ([Made-in-China](https://www.made-in-china.com/products-search/hot-china-products/Adjustable_Dumbbell.html)). Badges prove little about who owns the factory. Alibaba's Gold Supplier status is "not a factory or quality audit" ([Tianwen](https://tianwenwangluo.com/en/is-alibaba-legit/)). Alibaba's tiers and 1688's "Super Factory" badge, about ¥106,800 a year, are memberships that suppliers buy ([Yakkyofy](https://yakkyofy.com/1688-vs-alibaba/)). Even third-party-audited pages contradict themselves. Hefei Bodyup reports 13 employees but claims output of 10,000–100,000 sets a month ([Made-in-China](https://bodyupsports.en.made-in-china.com/product/PfMYiKvhMFcw/China-Adjustable-Dumbbell-24kg-52-5lb-40kg-90lb-Gym-Dumbbell-Fitness-Equipment.html)). Nantong Get-Fit claims a 20,000 m² factory from an office-tower address ([Made-in-China](https://get-fit.en.made-in-china.com/product/JRUYzwdjnoWi/China-Gym-Equipment-24kg-40kg-Adjustable-Weights-Dumbbell-Sets-for-Body-Building.html)). Factory-direct is not automatically cheaper, either. For 24V kids' UTVs, the only workable price was US$117 from a trader at a Shenzhen office address ([Made-in-China](https://zhehuatoys.en.made-in-china.com/product/ZYnrUyqDrdWv/China-24V-Electric-Kids-Car-UTV-2-Seats-Children-Battery-Ride-on-Car.html)). Genuine Hebei and Jiaxing makers quoted US$150–178 for larger units that lose money at any price that undercuts Amazon.

A sound check runs in order of cost. It starts with a listing whose audit report names the auditor and carries a **report ID you can confirm on the auditor's own site**; TÜV Rheinland, SGS, Bureau Veritas and CTI all appear on Made-in-China pages. The next step is the business licence. Its 18-digit Unified Social Credit Code can be looked up free on **GSXT, China's official company registry**, where the business scope should include production verbs (生产, 制造, 加工) rather than only sales or wholesale, the status should be active, the insured-staff count should fit the claimed plant, and the registered address should sit in the product's cluster ([NewBuyingAgent](https://www.newbuyingagent.com/resources/how-to-read-a-chinese-business-license-and-verify-a-supplier)). The contract counterparty matters as much as the factory, because many genuine factories invoice through a sister trading company; Tengtai, for example, contracts through Nantong T&T International ([Made-in-China](https://tt-sports.en.made-in-china.com/product/iJXrVNvHZUWc/China-Gym-Home-Multi-Functional-Fitness-Equipment-Power-Rack-Half-Rack-Squat-Cage.html)). The last steps are a live video walk-through of the named production line and, before the first container, an **independent audit at US$210–290 per man-day** ([Supplyia](https://www.supplyia.com/inspection-company-in-china/)).

1688 runs a similar test itself. Its 源头工厂 ("source factory") badge requires a production-type licence, checked in real time against GSXT. Its on-site certification films the workshop and reviews three months of utility bills and social-insurance payments ([1688](https://wiki.1688.com/zh/WKfl9os8222xhc)). Yet "Super Factory" badges also appear on shops only one or two years old.

No official, verified buyer-side API exists for Alibaba, 1688, Made-in-China or Global Sources. Alibaba's and 1688's open platforms require developer approval, and 1688's requires Chinese enterprise credentials. Alibaba's robots.txt also disallows its trade-search path for all automated agents ([Alibaba](https://www.alibaba.com/robots.txt)). In access tests, Made-in-China was the only major platform that returned supplier-level records without a login or bot challenge. 1688, DHgate, Global Sources, ImportYeti and the Chinese registries all returned challenges, blocks or region restrictions. The cheapest workable stack is therefore mostly manual and nearly free.

1688, China's domestic wholesale site, lists comparable basic models 30–70% below export prices. Walking pads, for example, show ¥117–347 there against US$52–130 on export platforms ([1688](https://wiki.1688.com/zh/fitness/WKg04vsn5aicxs)). But those card prices are for the cheapest variant and exclude VAT, minimum order and export terms. Most 1688 micro-factories neither export nor hold Australian certifications. Foreign buyers need an agent, whose published commissions run 2–15%, most often 5–10% ([Sino-Shipping](https://www.sino-shipping.com/sv/inkop-upphandling-kina/hitta-leverantorer/basta-1688-agenter/)). They also need an exporter of record that can obtain the ChAFTA certificate on which 0% duty depends. After those costs the realistic saving at Australian order sizes is an estimated 10–30%. That matters for chairs or walking pads, but it is secondary to choosing the right product. Anyone automating supplier research should note that one Made-in-China product page embedded text aimed at automated extraction tools. Supplier pages fed into an AI pipeline must be treated as untrusted data, not instructions.

## Conclusion

The arbitrage is real but narrower and more physical than "Amazon minus 20%" suggests. Every product that survives is bulky enough to defeat Australia Post, simple enough to escape mandatory standards and electrical certification, and valuable enough per cubic metre that freight does not eat the margin. The binding competitor is not Amazon but the mass-retail house brand: Super Retail Group's free Club prices, Aldi Special Buys, Kmart and Bunnings. These periodically reset the floor, and they cut the first-pass margins on recovery kits, pressure washers, pizza ovens and dog crates. The fastest-selling categories and the profitable ones barely overlap, because strong demand is exactly what drew in the container-scale importers who now set the floor. The trampoline is the exception: its size keeps online sellers' delivered prices high, at roughly A$470–510 for a 14 ft model once an estimated A$80–120 delivery is added.

The economics reward container scale, and container scale multiplies the one risk nobody can measure from outside: how many bulky units one seller can move by local pickup in a season. The trampoline container holds 128 units and the hammock trial 200, and the base mixed gym container recovers its cash only after about 78% of its stock sells. That argues for buying information before buying inventory. A logged-in sweep of competing Marketplace listings in two or three cities, a small paid-ad demand test, firm quotes and samples from Lingsha, Tengtai or Ironman, and Xusheng, GSXT checks and one lab quote together cost hundreds to low thousands of dollars and resolve the largest unknowns. By contrast, a container costing A$28–53k that sells slowly ties up capital through a season of storage at A$273–389 a month for a single 21 m² unit ([Inside Self Storage](https://www.insideselfstorage.com/self-storage-industry-news/spaceout-conducts-survey-of-australia-self-storage-prices-reveals-results)).
